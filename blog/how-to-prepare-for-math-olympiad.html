How to Prepare Your Child for Math Olympiad

📅 For parents of Grade 3–8 students · 5 min read

Many parents hear about Math Olympiad exams and immediately feel nervous — both for themselves and their child. The truth is, preparing for Olympiad does not have to be stressful. With the right approach and a little daily practice, most children can do well without burning out.

This guide covers what Olympiad math actually tests, how to build a simple practice routine, and what genuinely helps versus what wastes time.

What does the Math Olympiad actually test?

Unlike school exams that test whether your child memorised a formula, Olympiad exams test whether your child can think through a problem. The questions often look unfamiliar — even if the underlying math is simple. Common topics include:

  • Number patterns and sequences (e.g. "what comes next: 2, 5, 10, 17, ?")
  • Basic geometry (areas, perimeters, angles)
  • Word problems involving time, speed, money, and fractions
  • Logical reasoning (odd-one-out, direction-based questions)
  • Simple algebra for older grades (finding unknown values)

The good news: none of this is beyond what your child learns in school. The difference is just practice with thinking, not memorising.

How much time does preparation actually take?

For most children, 15–20 minutes of daily practice for 4–6 weeks before the exam is enough. More than that often leads to frustration rather than improvement, especially for younger kids.

Consistency matters more than marathon study sessions. A child who practices 15 minutes every day for a month will almost always outperform one who does two hours the night before.

💡 Tip: Pick a fixed time each day — right after school snack, or just before dinner. Routine removes the daily negotiation about whether to study.

What kind of practice actually helps?

The most effective practice for Olympiad is solving problems you haven't seen before. This is different from homework, which usually practices techniques already taught in class.

Here is what works:

  • Pattern questions daily — even 5 minutes of "what comes next" sequences builds the kind of thinking Olympiad tests
  • Word problems with real-world context — money, distances, ages, sharing things equally
  • Timed practice — Olympiad exams have time limits, so practicing with a gentle timer helps children manage pace
  • Reviewing wrong answers together — understanding why an answer was wrong is more valuable than getting ten more questions right

What grade should a child start preparing from?

Most Olympiad competitions accept entries from Grade 2 or 3 onwards. However, there is no real benefit in starting intensive preparation before Grade 3. Before that age, the most useful "preparation" is simply encouraging your child to enjoy numbers — through games, puzzles, and casual counting activities.

For Grade 3–5 students, focus on number patterns, basic word problems, and logical reasoning. For Grade 6–8, add simple algebra, geometry, and percentage-based problems.

Common mistakes parents make

  • Buying too many books — one good workbook is enough. Jumping between multiple books creates confusion and wastes money.
  • Treating every wrong answer as a failure — wrong answers are information. They show exactly what to work on next.
  • Focusing only on speed — accuracy first, speed second. A child who solves 8 questions correctly beats one who rushes through 15 and gets half wrong.
  • Starting too close to the exam — two weeks of cramming is far less effective than six weeks of relaxed, daily practice.

💡 Tip: After your child finishes a practice session, ask them to explain one problem to you in their own words. If they can explain it, they understand it. If they can't, that is the question to revisit tomorrow.

One last thing

Results in Olympiad exams are less important than the thinking habits your child builds along the way. A child who learns to read a problem carefully, try different approaches, and stay calm when stuck — that child will do well in school exams, entrance tests, and real-life situations regardless of their Olympiad rank.

Keep the preparation light, consistent, and as stress-free as possible. That is the real preparation that lasts.
