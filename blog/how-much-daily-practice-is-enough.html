How Much Daily Practice Is Enough? A Grade-wise Guide for Parents

📅 For all school parents · 5 min read

One of the most common questions parents ask is: how much should my child study each day? The honest answer is that it depends on the child's age and grade — and that more is not always better.

Pushing a child to study for hours when they are not absorbing anything is one of the most common and most avoidable mistakes in school education. This guide gives practical, realistic numbers by grade, and explains why those numbers make sense.

The basic principle: quality over quantity

A child who studies with full attention for 30 minutes will almost always learn more than one who sits at a desk for 2 hours while being distracted. Attention span, not time spent, is what determines how much actually gets retained.

This is why the recommendations below are shorter than many parents expect. They represent focused, active study — not passive reading or mindless copying.

These are guidelines for practice and revision — they are in addition to homework assigned by the school, not instead of it. If your child's school homework already exceeds these numbers, you do not need to add more.

💡 Tip: These numbers assume breaks. A 45-minute session works better as three 15-minute blocks with a 5-minute break between each than as a straight 45 minutes. Younger children especially need these breaks to maintain focus.

Why less time often produces better results

There is a concept in learning called "spaced repetition" — the idea that studying something for 10 minutes today, 10 minutes tomorrow, and 10 minutes the day after produces far stronger memory than studying for 30 minutes in one sitting.

This is why a child who practices multiplication tables for 5 minutes every morning for a month will recall them far more reliably in an exam than one who spent 2 hours on them the week before. Frequency beats duration, especially for younger children.

Signs that your child is studying too much

More practice is not always better. Here are signs that the study load is too high for your child's age:

  • Frequent headaches or complaints of tiredness during study time
  • Losing interest in subjects they previously enjoyed
  • Grades not improving despite more study time
  • Difficulty sleeping because of worry about school
  • No time for physical activity or unstructured play

If any of these are happening, reducing study time and improving focus is usually more effective than adding more hours.

Signs that your child needs more practice

On the other side, some children do need more practice than others. Look for:

  • Consistently struggling with concepts weeks after they were taught in class
  • Very slow reading speed for their grade level
  • Unable to recall multiplication tables by Grade 4
  • Making the same mistakes repeatedly despite correction

If these signs are present, the answer is usually not more time but different kinds of practice — more active, more hands-on, and with more frequent review of weak areas specifically.

💡 Tip: If your child consistently gets a certain type of question wrong, that is not a reason to spend more time overall — it is a reason to spend specifically more time on that weak area while reducing time on things they already know well.

The role of short, daily games

One effective addition for most grade levels is a short daily activity that feels more like a game than studying. Mental maths questions, spelling practice, or science quizzes for 10–15 minutes — done at a relaxed pace with no exam pressure — build skills without adding to the stress of formal study.

One practical suggestion

The most useful thing most parents can do is not to add more study hours but to make the existing study time more focused. Remove distractions (phone, TV), set a clear end time in advance so the child knows when they will be done, and let them take real breaks between subjects.

A child who studies with full focus for 40 minutes is doing more than one who sits at a desk for 2 hours half-distracted. Focus and consistency, not hours, are what produce results over time.
